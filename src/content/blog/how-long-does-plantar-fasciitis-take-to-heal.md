**Short answer:** most people with plantar fasciitis start to feel better within a few weeks to a few months once they treat it. Some stubborn cases take six to twelve months. The sooner you start care, the faster you tend to heal.

This guide gives you a simple timeline, the things that slow healing down, and the signs that it is time to see a podiatrist.

## What is plantar fasciitis?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue under your foot. It runs from your heel bone to your toes and supports your arch. When this band gets irritated or strained, you feel pain in the heel or the bottom of the foot. The pain is often worst with the first steps in the morning or after you have been sitting for a while.

If this sounds like you, our page on [plantar fasciitis](/conditions/plantar-fasciitis-chronic-plantar-fasciosis/) explains the symptoms and causes in more detail.

## A simple recovery timeline

Every foot is different, so treat this as a general guide and not a promise.

- **Week 1 to 2:** You start to calm things down. Rest from the activity that hurt, wear supportive shoes, stretch your calves and arches, and ice the heel. Pain is often still there, but it should not be getting worse.
- **Week 3 to 6:** Many people notice that morning pain is less sharp and that they can walk longer. Keep stretching, even when you feel better.
- **Month 2 to 3:** If you have been steady with your care, pain is often much better. This is a good time to slowly return to normal exercise.
- **Month 6 to 12:** Some people still have pain at this point. That does not mean you will always have it. It means your care plan needs a closer look, and a podiatrist can help.

## What helps you heal faster?

- **Stretch every day.** Calf stretches and plantar fascia stretches take only a few minutes. Do them before you get out of bed and after long periods of sitting. See our [5 best exercises for plantar fasciitis](/blog/5-best-exercises-to-relieve-plantar-fasciitis).
- **Wear supportive shoes.** Choose shoes with good arch support and a firm heel. Try not to walk barefoot on hard floors, even at home.
- **Cut back, do not stop moving.** Lower the miles or the hours on your feet for a while. Swap running for swimming or cycling until the pain settles.
- **Try orthotics.** Arch supports or [custom orthotics](/blog/treating-your-plantar-fasciitis-with-orthotics) can take strain off the band.
- **Use ice and short-term pain relief.** Ice for about 20 minutes can help. Ask your doctor before you take anti-inflammatory medicine for more than a few days.
- **Try night splints or physical therapy.** These help if the pain is worst in the morning or if tight calves are part of the problem.

For more home ideas, read [tips for easing plantar fasciitis pain at home](/blog/tips-for-easing-your-plantar-fasciitis-pain-at-home).

## What slows healing down?

- Going back to running or long walks too soon
- Standing on hard floors all day for work
- Tight calf muscles
- Worn-out shoes with no support
- Extra body weight, which adds load to the heel
- Ignoring the pain until it has been there for months

## When should you see a podiatrist?

Book a visit if:

- Heel pain lasts more than two weeks
- The pain is getting worse, or it wakes you at night
- You change the way you walk to avoid the pain
- You notice swelling, numbness or tingling
- You cannot put weight on the foot (this needs care right away)

At your visit, we find out whether the pain really is plantar fasciitis. Heel pain can also come from a [heel spur](/conditions/heel-spurs/), a nerve problem or a stress fracture, and each needs a different plan. Read more in [when plantar fasciitis pain does not go away](/blog/when-plantar-fasciitis-pain-doesnt-go-away).

## What can a podiatrist add to your care?

If home care is not enough, your podiatrist may suggest custom orthotics, taping or strapping, a short time in a walking boot, physical therapy, a steroid injection in selected cases, or [shockwave therapy](/blog/heal-plantar-fasciitis-pain-with-shockwave-therapy). Surgery is rare. It is only considered when several months of treatment have not worked.
